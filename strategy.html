How to Attempt the Paper

Order, arithmetic and revision priorities for the three-hour CSIR NET Mathematical Sciences paper

Built from the marking scheme, not from folklore

The arithmetic of guessing

Before any advice about topics, settle what a guess is worth. Let $p$ be your probability of getting a question right.

Part B: $+3$ for a right answer, $-0.75$ for a wrong one

The expected gain from attempting is

$$E_B(p) = 3p - 0.75(1-p) = 3.75p - 0.75,$$

which is positive exactly when $p > 0.2$. A blind guess among four options gives $p = 0.25$ and $E_B = 0.1875$ — technically positive, but so small that a handful of such guesses is statistical noise. Eliminating even one option lifts $p$ to $1/3$ and $E_B$ to $0.5$; eliminating two gives $p = 0.5$ and $E_B = 1.125$.

Rule for Part B. Attempt if you can rule out at least one option. Skip only if all four look equally plausible and you already have 25 better attempts.

Part C: $+4.75$ for a fully correct response, nothing deducted

Here $E_C(p) = 4.75p \ge 0$ for every $p$. There is no value of $p$ at which leaving a counted attempt blank is better than filling it.

But "correct" means the exact subset. With four options and no idea at all, you are choosing among $2^4 - 1 = 15$ non-empty subsets, so a truly blind $p \approx 1/15$. Deciding each option independently is far better: if you can settle three of the four options confidently and are guessing on one, $p = 0.5$.

The Part C trap. Marks are awarded only for the complete correct subset. Selecting three of four correct options scores zero — identical to selecting three wrong ones. Treat each option as a separate true/false question and only submit when you have a verdict on every one.

A time budget that works

Three hours is 180 minutes for 60 counted attempts — three minutes per attempt on average, but the parts are not equally expensive.

PhaseMinutesWhat you do
Part A25–30All 20 questions read, best 15 attempted. Aptitude questions are either quick or not worth the fight.
Part B, first pass45Answer only what you recognise on sight. Mark the rest for review; do not stop to derive.
Part C, first pass55Same discipline. Look for questions where each option is a short independent check.
Second pass40Return to marked questions, in the part where your attempt count is still short of the cap.
Reserve10Fill every counted Part C slot. There is no penalty for being wrong there.
Why Part A goes first. It carries 30 marks with no subject knowledge and the shortest solutions in the paper. Doing it while you are freshest converts the easiest marks in the paper before fatigue sets in.

What to revise, and in what order

There is no per-unit quota in Parts B and C. You may answer entirely from the units you know. That makes depth in three areas worth more than shallow coverage of all four.

  1. Linear algebra — rank, eigenvalues, diagonalisability, Jordan form, quadratic-form signature. Almost every question is settled by computation, so it rewards practice rather than insight.
  2. Real analysis — convergence tests, uniform convergence, continuity and differentiability counterexamples. Part C loves "which of the following are true" questions built from standard counterexamples; collect them.
  3. Group and ring theory — orders of elements, Sylow counting, ideals in $\mathbb{Z}_n$ and in polynomial rings, irreducibility. High density of short questions.
  4. Complex analysis — residues, singularity classification, Möbius maps. A small syllabus with a large question share.
  5. ODEs and numerical analysis — existence-uniqueness conditions, Sturm–Liouville, Newton–Raphson and Runge–Kutta orders of convergence. Formulaic and quick.
  6. Unit IV — for anyone from a statistics background this is the cheapest unit in the paper: probability inequalities, standard distributions, estimation and testing, linear models, Markov chains and queueing all appear directly.
Counterexamples are the currency of Part C. Keep a single page of them: a continuous nowhere-differentiable function, a convergent-but-not-absolutely convergent series, a non-normal subgroup, a ring that is a UFD but not a PID, a bounded sequence with no convergent subsequence in an infinite-dimensional space. Most "which of these statements are true" questions are decided by one of them.

Working through the solved papers on this site

Use each paper twice. The first time, keep every solution collapsed, sit with a clock and attempt it as an exam under the real caps: 15, 25 and 20 attempts. Only then open the solutions.

The second time, a fortnight later, read the solutions of the questions you got wrong and write out the "Idea used" line for each in your own words. If you cannot state which theorem a question tests, you have not yet learned it, however clear the algebra looked.

The Reveal every solution button prints the whole paper with solutions open, which is what you want for the second pass. The topic filter shows one topic at a time when you are revising a single area.
